Irrigation Electrical Troubleshooting

Traditional (non‑2‑wire) controllers

Before you use this guide

You do not need to be an irrigation technician to use this guide, but you do need to be safe around electricity. It assumes you understand basic electrical safety and can take a reading without putting yourself at risk. It is not a course in electrical work.

A controller is connected to line voltage even with the dial off. The 24 volt common is not a neutral and can be energized on older or ungrounded installations. Read the Safety notes section at the bottom of the guide before handling bare conductors.

Equipment, wiring practice, and local code vary. Verify every reading and every conclusion against the equipment in front of you and the manufacturer's documentation. The low voltage side is irrigation work; the line side of the transformer is electrical work. If you are not qualified for it, stop and get someone who is.

If you are not confident taking readings on a controller that is connected to power, stop here and call someone who is.

Safety notes

Line voltage is present with the dial off

Turning the dial to off stops the stations. It does not disconnect the controller from line voltage — the transformer stays energized. Anything you open inside the enclosure should be treated as live until you have confirmed otherwise.

The common is not a neutral

The 24 VAC common has no guaranteed relationship to earth ground. On an older controller it can sit at line potential, and there is enough behind it to hurt you badly.

Several things cause it. A transformer with failed insulation between the primary and secondary windings bridges line voltage onto the 24 volt side. A two‑prong cord, or an outlet with no equipment ground, leaves nothing to clamp the common near ground and nothing to clear a fault through. Line conductors landed backward put the hot where the neutral belongs.

With a proper ground present, the same fault usually either holds the common near ground or draws enough current to trip the breaker. Without one, the first path to earth can be whoever is touching the wire. That is why the same model of controller will bite you at one house and not at the next — the controller is the same, the building wiring is not.

Check to ground, not just across terminals

Terminal to terminal reads 24 volts whether or not the common is energized, so it tells you nothing about this fault. Before handling bare conductors, read from each terminal — including the common — to a known earth ground.

If you find voltage on the common to ground

Stop and remove power at the controller before working on splices. Unplug it, or disconnect it carefully at the controller if it is hardwired.

Record the programs first. On an older controller without battery backup, pulling power wipes the schedule, and a schedule rebuilt wrong is a callback and an annoyed customer. Photograph the settings or write them down before you cut power, not after.

This is also the reason not to kill power as a routine step. Do it when you have a reason to, and know what it costs when you do.

Tools

Never touch a bare conductor with an uninsulated tool. A screwdriver with a worn or missing handle, or a pair of pliers with the grips gone, turns a survivable contact into a serious one.

Know where this stops being your work

The low voltage side is irrigation work. The line side of the transformer is electrical work. If you are not qualified for it, stop there and get someone who is.

Meter types and dial symbols

Which meter you are holding

Digital, autoranging. The most common field meter. You pick volts or ohms and it sorts out the scale itself. Watch the unit on the display, because it changes the scale mid-reading without telling you.

Digital, manual range. You pick both the function and the range. If you set a range lower than the actual value it reads OL, which looks identical to an open circuit. If a reading seems impossible, check the range before you condemn the wire.

Analog, needle. Older and still in plenty of trucks. Harder to read precisely, but the needle shows movement a digital display averages away — which makes it genuinely better for spotting fluctuating voltage on a chattering valve. If you have one, it is worth keeping for exactly that.

Clamp meter. Reads current through a wire without breaking the circuit. Most also do volts and ohms with leads. The clamp itself is of limited use at 24 volts, since the current draw is small and many clamps will not resolve it.

Non-contact voltage pen. Not a meter. It tells you a live conductor is nearby and nothing more. It will not reliably sense 24 volts, and it will not tell you a number. Do not troubleshoot a zone with one.

Buttons vary but the common ones are HOLD, which freezes the reading so you can pull the leads out of an awkward box and then look, and RANGE, which steps an autoranging meter through the scales manually.

The jacks

COM takes the black lead and stays there for everything.

VΩ takes the red lead for volts, ohms, and continuity. This is where it lives for all of this work.

A or 10A is the current jack. Nothing in this guide needs it. A red lead left in that jack while you measure voltage puts a near short across the circuit and will take out the meter's fuse, or worse.

What the display is telling you

OL, or 1 with nothing after it, or a blank leading digit: the value is past what the meter can read on its current setting. On ohms that usually means an open. On a manual-range meter it may just mean the range is set too low.

AUTO means the meter is picking its own scale. HOLD means you are looking at a frozen number, not a live one — easy to forget and then misread three zones in a row.

A minus sign on DC just means the leads are reversed. On AC it will not appear, because polarity does not apply.

The battery symbol is late warning. Drifting or slow readings show up well before it does, so if numbers will not settle, change the battery before you chase the fault.

Using your meter

Before you touch anything

Set the dial to what you intend to measure before you put the leads on the circuit. Most meters that die in the field die because they were still set to ohms or amps when somebody probed a live terminal.

Keep the leads in the two right-hand jacks for everything in this guide — black in COM, red in the jack marked for volts and ohms. The amps jack is a different hole and you will not need it here. A lead left in the amps jack while you read voltage puts a near short across whatever you touch.

Irrigation control wiring runs about 24 volts AC, which is low enough not to hurt you under normal conditions. The 120 volt side feeding the transformer is not. Do not open that side unless that is your trade.

What the three numbers mean

Everything in this guide comes down to three quantities and the relationship between them.

Voltage is the electrical push. It is what the controller sends down the zone wire to pull the solenoid in. You measure it with the circuit live and the meter across two points — the common and the zone.

Resistance is how much the path fights that push. You measure it with the circuit dead, and what you read is the whole loop: out along the zone wire, through the solenoid coil, back along the common.

Current is what actually flows once voltage is pushing against resistance. You will not measure it directly here, but it is why the other two matter. Too much resistance and not enough current flows to pull the solenoid in, even though your voltmeter reads fine with nothing connected.

That last point catches people. A run with a corroded splice can show good voltage when the meter is the only thing on the wire, then collapse the moment a solenoid tries to draw through it. Voltage measured under load is the honest number.

Measuring resistance

Turn the controller off first. Resistance is a dead-circuit measurement — a meter set to ohms on a live wire will read nonsense and may not survive it.

Touch the two leads together before you start. A good meter reads near zero. If it does not, your leads or the meter battery are suspect, and every reading after that is wrong.

At the controller, black on COM and red on the zone terminal. Out at the valve, straight across the two solenoid leads with the splice separated.

An open circuit — a cut wire, a burned coil — reads OL, or 1 with nothing after it, depending on the meter. That is the display saying the resistance is past what it can measure, not a low number.

Reading the prefix

The number on the screen means nothing without the unit next to it. A meter on autorange will switch scales on you mid-measurement.

No prefix is ohms. k is a thousand ohms. M is a million. A solenoid that should read 35 and reads 35 k is not slightly high — it is a thousand times high, and there is a break in the path.

Same on the voltage side: m in front of V is millivolts, thousandths. A reading of 400 mV is not 400 volts, it is four tenths of one.

Measuring AC voltage

Irrigation control circuits are alternating current, so use the VAC setting — the V with a wavy line, not the V with a straight or dashed line. A DC setting on an AC circuit will read low or wander.

The zone has to be commanded on before there is anything to read. Turn it on at the controller first.

Polarity does not matter on AC. Either lead on either point.

Around 24 to 30 volts at the terminal is a healthy controller output. Most solenoids need at least 18 to pull in. A number between those two is the interesting case: the controller is working and something between it and the valve is eating the difference.

Continuity

The beeper setting is a fast yes-or-no version of a resistance check. It sounds when the path is complete.

It is useful for finding which wire is which in a bundle, or confirming a splice you just made. It is not a substitute for reading actual resistance on a solenoid, because a coil that is partially shorted will still beep.

Mistakes worth avoiding

  • Measuring resistance with the controller still on.
  • Reading the number and not the unit.
  • Trusting a voltage reading taken with nothing connected. Load it and read again.
  • Probing through wire insulation instead of at a clean connection. It works until it does not, and it leaves a hole where water gets in.
  • Blaming the circuit for a dying meter battery. Low battery shows up as drifting and slow readings before the low-battery symbol does.
  • Testing at the controller only. Half the useful information comes from splitting the run at the valve.

Notes — read before starting
  • There are exceptions to every rule.
  • This guide does not cover every problem. There are too many possibilities.
  • Troubleshoot one zone at a time.
  • Exceptions to the 20–60 Ω range:
  • Multiple valves in parallel on one zone — reads lower.
  • Valves in series — reads much higher. You should not see this; it is not a correct way to run them.
  • Odd solenoids with higher or lower resistance, such as Toro spike guard.
